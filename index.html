01 / 06 · WRITING CODE

First, someone writes a button.

Every feature on your phone starts as instructions typed by a person. Before it goes near real users, automated checklists test it for mistakes.

Just like a pilot's pre-flight check, computers run thousands of simulated taps in minutes to verify the new button works—and didn't accidentally break older buttons nearby.
02 / 06 · RELEASING IN WAVES

Release in slow waves, never all at once.

You wouldn't serve soup to 500 wedding guests without tasting one spoonful first. Engineers roll out new changes in careful waves—starting with 1% of people, then 5%, 25%, 50%, and only then 100%.

Automated monitors spot the bump in errors immediately and pull the update back. The other 95% of people never even knew a new version existed. Engineers call this a progressive canary rollout.
03 / 06 · REAL BUILDINGS

"The cloud" is real buildings in separate cities.

Your photos and apps live inside real buildings full of computers spread across different cities. Being nearby makes apps feel instant—and keeps everything running if one city loses power.

Each building has its own power grid, cooling system, and fiber cables—like watertight compartments in a ship so one leak can't sink the whole vessel. Cloud engineers call these cities regions.
04 / 06 · ONE TAP

What happens in the blink after you tap.

When you tap "Send," your phone asks the internet's directory for the address, connects to the closest healthy building, and gets an answer back—usually in less than a tenth of a second.

Engineers call it DNS (the Domain Name System). Humans remember names like "instagram.com," while computers need number addresses—the directory translates one to the other every time.
05 / 06 · INVISIBLE FIRES

Small fires happen every single day.

Parts wear out and cables get snagged constantly inside giant data centers. Automated traffic steering notices in milliseconds and moves you to a healthy building—so you never feel a flicker.

Not to keep you online. Health checks tap each building on the shoulder every few seconds and steer traffic away automatically—giving engineers calm breathing room to repair the hardware.
06 / 06 · BREAKING CONTAINMENT

Why big outages still happen.

Backup buildings can't stop a bad update sent to every city at the same moment—or a directory that suddenly forgets the address. That's the rare moment an invisible fire reaches your screen.

In Google's Site Reliability Engineering handbook, 100% reliability is actually the wrong target—it freezes new improvements and costs far more than anyone on a cell signal can notice. Instead, teams learn from every fire without blame.
